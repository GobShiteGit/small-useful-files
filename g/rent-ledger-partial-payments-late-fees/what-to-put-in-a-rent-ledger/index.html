What should a rent ledger for a few units include?

A rent ledger needs two lists: what each tenant owes (charges) and what they paid (payments), with every payment tied to a charge. From those two lists you can work out any balance.

List 1: tenants

One row per tenant, with the unit label and the terms that apply to them. If you charge late fees, note each tenant's grace days, daily fee and cap, since these can differ by lease. Use one exact spelling of each tenant's name everywhere else.

List 2: charges

One row for each thing a tenant owes:

  • Charge ID: a short unique code, for example `A-2026-11` (tenant A, November 2026).
  • Tenant.
  • Description: November rent, repair bill, and so on.
  • Due date.
  • Amount.

Charge the rent every month as a separate row. A mid-month move-in gets its own prorated charge.

List 3: payments

One row for each payment received:

  • Date received.
  • Charge ID it pays.
  • Amount.
  • Method: cash, check, transfer.
  • Note: for example, partial, rest due on the 20th.

What you can calculate from these lists

  • Paid so far on a charge: add up the payments with that charge ID.
  • Balance: charge amount minus paid so far.
  • Date fully paid: the date of the payment that brought the balance to zero.
  • Per tenant: total charged, total paid and total balance.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

Charges for Tenant A: Oct rent 1000, Nov rent 1000, Dec rent 1000. Payments: 600 on Oct 10 and 400 on Oct 20 (both for the October charge), and 500 on Nov 3 (November charge).

  • October: paid 1000, balance 0.
  • November: paid 500, balance 500.
  • December: paid 0, balance 1000.
  • Total charged 3000, total paid 1500, total balance 1500.

Keep late fees separate from rent

If you charge late fees, track them in their own figures rather than mixing them into the rent amount. Then you can see rent owed and fees owed separately, and record when a fee was collected or waived. Whether and how you may charge late fees depends on your lease and where you live, so check the current rules yourself.

Mistakes that break the totals

  1. Typing a tenant's name two different ways. Payments and charges stop lining up.
  2. Duplicate charge IDs. Two charges sharing an ID mix their payments together.
  3. Payments with no charge ID, or the wrong one.
  4. One payment covering two months entered as one row. Split it into one row per charge.
  5. Payments entered out of date order. The running balance and the date fully paid get confusing.
  6. Inserting rows in the middle of a spreadsheet. This can break formulas. Add new rows at the bottom instead.
  7. Overpayments left unnoticed. Add a check that flags when payments exceed the charge.

Keep a backup

Save a copy of the file before you start typing real data, and another copy now and then as you go.

What a ledger does not replace

A rent ledger records money owed and received. It is not a tax record, an expense tracker or legal advice. Keep those separate and check the rules where you live.
